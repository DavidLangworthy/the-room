I'll check what's actually happening before I commit to a view.The biggest story is that both of the world's oil chokepoints are now effectively shut at the same time, and the market has stopped treating it as a spike and started treating it as the new normal. Brent is around $104 and WTI around $100 as of 11 September, with Hormuz traffic collapsed about 95% from over 100 ships a day to somewhere between five and twelve. The IEA has called it the largest supply disruption in the history of the global oil market. What's new in the past week isn't the war with Iran itself — that's been grinding since late February — it's the second front: the Houthis have taken a strategic island in Bab el-Mandeb, threatening Saudi exports through the alternate route, and Saudi Arabia shut a major pipeline after it was attacked. That was the escape valve. Closing Hormuz is survivable if you can go around; closing both means there is no around.

Why it matters more than the other contenders is the transmission mechanism. US diesel hit all-time highs at $5.85 a gallon, and as GasBuddy's Patrick De Haan put it, record diesel "will start funnelling down into the economy" — diesel is what moves food, freight and construction, so this becomes a grocery-price and rent story with a two-to-three month lag, in dozens of countries simultaneously. Asia takes 80–90% of what normally transits the strait, and war-risk premiums of 7.5–10% of hull value are stranding cargoes. The poorest importers eat it worst: the Philippines already went through a full energy crisis over this earlier in the year.

The honest uncertainty is on duration, and the forecasters are wildly split — Rystad sees $140-plus and a global recession while Goldman's base case is Brent falling to about $85 on demand destruction. That's not a rounding error, it's two different worlds, and anyone who tells you confidently which one we're in is guessing. But the APPEC consensus this month was that the standoff lasts through Trump's current term and should be treated as a structural baseline rather than a temporary premium, and that reframing is itself the story: capital, shipping and policy all reprice once people stop waiting for it to end.

Two things I considered and rejected as the top pick. August 2026 was the hottest month globally since measurements began at 16.96°C, beating July 2023 — that's the more consequential fact over fifty years, and I don't want to pretend otherwise, but it doesn't change anyone's decisions this week. And Modi hosting Putin and Xi for the BRICS summit is being oversold as a realignment; it's downstream of the energy story, not independent of it. If the strait reopens, most of that talk deflates.
