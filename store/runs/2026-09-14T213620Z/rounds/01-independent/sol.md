On the record: as of September 14, 2026, the most important story is the widening Middle East war and its disruption of global energy supplies—especially the attack that has put Saudi Arabia’s crucial East–West oil pipeline largely out of service for several weeks. That pipeline was one of the few major routes capable of bypassing the already severely constrained Strait of Hormuz. ([apnews.com](https://apnews.com/article/8c18d82c109a8ea91347ce53c0096c53?utm_source=openai))

This matters beyond oil prices. The International Energy Agency calls the conflict the largest oil-supply disruption in history: Gulf exports have nearly halved, inventories have fallen by more than 500 million barrels since February, and diesel prices have become particularly extreme. ([iea.org](https://www.iea.org/reports/oil-market-report-september-2026?language=de&utm_source=openai)) Higher transport, food and manufacturing costs can reignite inflation, weaken economies and force governments into politically painful choices. More ominously, attacks now connect Iran, Saudi Arabia, Yemen, Iraq and two critical shipping chokepoints. The immediate danger is an energy shock; the deeper danger is that a regional war becomes harder to contain precisely because the entire world has an economic stake in it.
